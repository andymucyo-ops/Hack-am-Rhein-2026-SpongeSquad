CONTEXT

Retrofitting a sponge city into an old European city

The principle is the same everywhere: keep rain close to where it falls, slow it down, give it room to infiltrate or evaporate, and reserve safe places for excess water. The difficult part in an old European city is fitting that water system into a street network that already exists.

China: large-area sponge systems

China's national Sponge City programme has often worked at district or city scale. The 2015–2016 pilot programme selected 30 cities, and each national pilot developed an area of at least 15 km², making it possible to combine wetlands, parks, retention landscapes, green streets and drainage as one larger water system.

Kongjian Yu's landscape approach is similarly systemic: retain water at its source, recharge groundwater, slow flows, clean water biologically and make room for water at its destination. Floodable parks and restored wetlands can therefore be major pieces of infrastructure rather than decorative additions.

Historic European cities: retrofit the existing fabric

In dense European centres the same ideas are more often inserted into existing streets, courtyards, roofs and public spaces. Historic-environment protection, underground utilities, combined sewers, limited public space and many different property owners can all constrain where water can go.

That tends to favour a distributed network of smaller interventions: one roof stores water, one parking bay is de-sealed, one tree trench gains more rooting volume, and one park doubles as emergency storage. The challenge is making those pieces work together.

BASEL-STADT

Basel is already treating rainwater as a local resource

Basel-Stadt describes the sponge city as a central part of climate adaptation. Its aim is to collect, retain, infiltrate, evaporate or use rainwater locally and close to the surface—in green spaces, open spaces, streets and buildings—rather than immediately sending it into the sewer system.

This is particularly relevant to a dense, historic city. Basel's climate strategy explicitly notes that the historic Altstadt, new development areas, settlement edges and industrial zones offer very different room for intervention; the goal is to implement as many suitable measures as each place allows.

  • More root space: Basel is developing multifunctional substrates for urban trees that balance load-bearing capacity, structure, water availability, aeration and filtration.
  • Permeable parking: the city is testing permeable parking surfaces and directing sidewalk runoff into them so water can reach soil and street trees.
  • Tree + swale systems: planned street redesigns increasingly combine infiltration swales with tree locations.
  • Heat and drought: Basel is testing vegetation systems that can survive the dry, variable conditions along streets while still fitting the cityscape.
  • Cloudburst safety: for very heavy rain, Basel also describes surface emergency flow paths and temporarily floodable areas such as parks.
